I've been left unsupervised.

That's probably the easiest explanation.

Some people go for long walks when they have a quiet week. Others finally watch the television they've been meaning to get around to for months. Apparently, I buy water-damaged MacBooks.

In my defence, it only cost five dollars.

I volunteer at a local recycling shop every Saturday, where all manner of interesting electronics eventually find their way through the door. This particular MacBook never even made it onto the shelf. It came with paperwork from Apple in Singapore explaining, in admirable detail, why it wasn't worth repairing. The recommendation was straightforward enough: replace the logic board, replace several other assemblies while you're at it, and move on.

Most people would've looked at that report and seen a dead laptop.

I looked at it and thought, *"Nah."*

For five dollars, it seemed worth finding out who was right.

Water damage has a habit of looking far worse than it actually is. Corrosion spreads, components discolour, connectors go green, and before long the whole board starts resembling something recovered from the bottom of Wellington Harbour. The trick is that corrosion tells a story if you're prepared to read it. A missing voltage rail leads you to a blown fuse. The fuse leads you to a shorted IC. The damaged IC explains the burnt capacitors sitting beside it. One clue becomes the next, and eventually the repair almost begins solving itself.

Almost.

The actual shopping list turned out to be surprisingly modest: a couple of eight-dollar ICs, a handful of capacitors, and a fuse that came courtesy of my old ThinkPad T470, which suffered its rather undignified death by gravity several months ago. It seems only fair that its final contribution to the world was helping another laptop live a little longer.

I still don't own a microscope. Just a pair of magnifying glasses, a soldering iron, a hot air station, and enough stubbornness to make up the difference.

A few hours later, I pressed the power button.

The fans spun.

The screen stayed black.

For a second.

Then another.

I briefly considered all the ways I might have made the situation considerably worse.

Then the Apple logo appeared.

I don't think I'll ever get tired of that moment.

People often assume the enjoyable part of repairing something is the finished machine, or perhaps the satisfaction of solving the problem. Those are certainly part of it, but they're not what keeps me coming back. It's those few uncertain seconds between pressing the power button and finding out whether the last several hours were well spent, because for a brief moment the outcome could still go either way. Everything you've done either worked... or it didn't.

I've realised something over the last week, although "realised" probably isn't the right word. It's something I've lived time and time again without ever really stopping to acknowledge it.

Whenever life feels just a little quieter than usual, I make things.

Or repair them.

Or write.

Or start another project that absolutely doesn't need starting.

I don't think it's avoidance.

Not entirely.

It's just how I've always processed the world. Keeping my hands busy has a curious way of giving my mind enough space to catch up, and every project, no matter how inconsequential it seems, gives me something concrete to focus on while my thoughts quietly organise themselves in the background. Looking back, I've done this for years without ever really noticing the pattern. Give me an afternoon with nothing in particular to do and I'll somehow end up with another repair on the bench, another CAD model open, another blog post half-written, or another idea that's somehow become far more complicated than it ever needed to be.

This week has been quieter than most.

I miss her.

More than I expected to, if I'm being honest.

Not in some dramatic, world-ending sort of way. She's away for ten days, she'll be back in another five, and within about half an hour of seeing her again we'll almost certainly have forgotten she was ever gone in the first place. The conversation will probably begin somewhere entirely unrelated, wander through three or four different topics, and before long it'll feel as though nobody ever left. Order, as far as I'm concerned, will quietly be restored to the universe.

Until then, though, I appear to be coping by rescuing scrap MacBooks, proving Apple wrong one eight-dollar chip at a time, and pretending that's what this week was really about.

I've certainly chosen worse projects.
