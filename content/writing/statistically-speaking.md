It's mildly disconcerting, honestly.

Not dramatically so. She's only away for ten days, and by the time she's back I'll probably have repaired something that shouldn't have been repairable, broken something that was working perfectly happily beforehand, written another blog post, and somehow convinced myself that learning yet another obscure piece of technology is an excellent use of my time. Rationally, ten days is nothing.

Apparently my brain disagrees.

At precisely 9:58 this evening, my phone didn't ring.

Normally there's a reasonable chance it would. Not because we'd arranged anything, but because, statistically speaking, I was probably free. Likewise, somewhere around half past nine I found myself half expecting a message along the lines of, *"How free are you on a scale of one to America?"*

Instead, there wasn't one.

It's funny how quickly your brain learns patterns without ever consciously deciding to. You don't wake up one morning and think, *right, a phone call at roughly ten o'clock has become part of my day*. It just... happens. One conversation becomes two, then twenty, then enough that your evenings quietly reshape themselves around the possibility of hearing your phone vibrate.

Then, one day, it doesn't.

Not because anything's wrong, or because anyone's upset. Simply because somebody's somewhere else for a little while.

The silence itself isn't loud.

It's just... noticeable.

I spend an unreasonable amount of time thinking about systems. Computers, radios, firmware—they're wonderfully predictable. Give them the same inputs and, assuming you've done your job properly, they'll generally produce the same outputs. If they don't, there's usually a reason, and with enough persistence you'll eventually find it.

People don't work like that.

They quietly become part of your routine without asking permission. You don't schedule it, you don't really notice it happening, and then one day you realise you've found something interesting and instinctively reached for your phone before remembering that they're currently several thousand kilometres away doing considerably more exciting things than reading about whatever obscure rabbit hole you've disappeared down this afternoon.

It's an odd feeling.

Not sadness.

Not loneliness.

Just... absence.

I think that's one of the stranger things about memory. We like to imagine that we remember the important moments: the milestones, the dramatic conversations, the life-changing events. I'm not convinced that's true. I think we remember habits instead—the ordinary things that quietly repeated often enough to become invisible. The phone call that usually happens. The message you almost expect. The person you automatically look towards before remembering they're somewhere else today.

Those are the things your brain reaches for first, not because they're extraordinary, but because they've quietly become normal.

That's why I write.

Not because I think these moments are particularly remarkable, but because I know I'd otherwise forget them. Nothing else is ever just about the main point I make, and apparently this post isn't really about a missing phone call either. It's about realising that somebody can become such a normal part of your day that their absence doesn't feel dramatic.

It just feels... statistically improbable.

The good news is that this really is a remarkably temporary anomaly.

In ten days she'll be back, there'll almost certainly be a phone call at some vaguely predictable time that starts on one topic before somehow ending up somewhere completely different, and within about five minutes the universe will have quietly corrected itself. This brief disruption to the statistical model of my evenings will disappear just as naturally as it arrived, and I suspect neither of us will think much about it ever again.

Order, as they say, will once again be restored to the universe.

Until then, though, it turns out that 9:50pm onwards has become a surprisingly interesting time of day.

Statistically speaking.

With love, Daniel
