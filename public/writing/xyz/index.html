It Was Supposed to Be Simple

August 30, 2026 · 8 min · 1667 words · Daniel Liddle

Apparently I’ve been busy.

I say apparently because when I look back over the last month, I can’t quite work out where all of it went. There have been phones to fix, English assignments to submit at least three days later than I was supposed to, exams to study for, a new monitor to acquire, a computer to rebuild, and somewhere in the middle of all of that I decided that what my life really needed was a fifty cubic centimetre engineering problem with two wheels.

The moped seemed like a good idea at the time.

This is becoming a recurring theme.

The last few weeks have been one of those periods where nothing individually feels particularly significant, but somehow everything happens at once. I’ve been fixing phones for people, studying for exams while simultaneously trying to convince myself that I had absolutely not left my English assignments until three days after the deadline, and putting together another computer after selling the old one to fund the purchase of something that I apparently decided was a sensible replacement for a functioning desktop.

Then I bought the moped.

It was cheap.

That should probably have been the first warning.

There is perhaps another reason I thought it was a good idea, and that is that I have developed a slightly dangerous amount of confidence in my ability to fix things.

Over the last year or so, I’ve had enough successes to make failure feel increasingly hypothetical. The Samsung S23 Ultra that had no business being recoverable somehow worked after the bypass. The ThinkPad BIOS problem that looked like a complete write-off turned into a working machine. The water-damaged MacBook Pro that Apple had effectively condemned came back to life for a handful of dollars and some stubbornness.

At some point, if enough things work, you start to believe that perhaps you are simply rather good at this.

That is a useful belief right up until you buy a project that turns out to be a complete money pit.

I am beginning to suspect that I am owed one.

The moped may be collecting that debt.

Buying an old moped is one of those things that sounds remarkably simple when you describe it in one sentence.

You buy the moped. You put fuel in it. You ride the moped.

Unfortunately, reality has discovered a much more interesting sequence of events.

First, I had to find the parts.

The particular engine in this thing isn’t exactly unusual, but apparently the exact combination of parts required to make mine work has become rare enough that finding them involves significantly more international logistics than I had anticipated. One particularly important part eventually turned up in England, which seemed excessive for something that had to travel halfway around the world simply so I could make a fifty cubic centimetre scooter move under its own power.

It arrived. It fitted. The moped ran. This was, obviously, the end of the problem…

It was not the end of the problem.

I bought a battery, got the registration sorted, arranged insurance, and finally reached the point where it looked like I might actually be able to ride the thing. I had reached that particularly satisfying stage of a project where everything seemed to be falling into place, and for a brief moment I could actually imagine myself simply getting on it and going somewhere rather than spending another evening searching for obscure engine parts.

Then it stalled while I was riding it.

Oh.

The battery was discharged.

That seemed reasonably straightforward, except the battery shouldn’t have been discharged in the first place, which meant that the charging system wasn’t doing what a charging system is supposed to do.

So began the next investigation.

Stator or regulator?

Regulator or stator?

Perhaps both?

I spent an unreasonable amount of time testing wires, measuring voltages, staring at wiring diagrams, and trying to determine whether the electrical system was actually charging the battery or merely pretending to.

Eventually, the stator appeared to be the culprit.

Excellent. Except I couldn’t find one. Of course I couldn’t.

The dimensions were just different enough from the common parts to make every search produce something that looked almost right until I actually measured it. Eighteen coils. Roughly the right diameter. Roughly the right mounting holes. Roughly the right centre opening.

“Roughly” is not particularly useful when you’re trying to bolt something to a spinning engine.

Eventually, after considerably more searching than I would have liked, I found one.

I bought it.

Now I’m waiting for it to arrive.

It could fix everything. It could fix nothing. The regulator could also be dead.

At this point I’m essentially waiting for a piece of copper wire wrapped around an iron core to tell me whether I’ve wasted another week.

The funny thing is that none of this is particularly unusual for me.

I think I’ve just become accustomed to projects behaving like this, partly because I’ve spent enough time fixing things that I now expect there to be another problem hiding underneath the first one, and partly because every successful repair makes it slightly easier to convince myself that the next impossible thing will probably work too.

A phone arrives with a broken screen and turns out to have three other faults.

A computer needs one component replaced and somehow ends up completely disassembled across my desk.

A moped needs a part and suddenly I’m comparing stator dimensions between engines I’ve never even seen in person.

There is always another layer. Sometimes that’s the fun of it. Sometimes it is considerably less fun.

And sometimes I look at the pile of parts on my desk and wonder why I voluntarily do this to myself.

The answer, unfortunately, is usually that I wanted to know what would happen.

Somewhere in amongst all of this, I’ve also been doing the things I’m technically supposed to be doing.

School, for example. Theoretically, I am a student. Practically, I seem to be someone who occasionally remembers that assignments have deadlines and exams require studying.

I’ve handed in English work late enough that calling it “late” has started to feel optimistic, although I did eventually hand it in, which I think deserves at least partial credit for persistence. I’ve been studying too, although there is something deeply ironic about spending several hours learning chemistry while simultaneously thinking about whether a stator from a completely different engine might physically fit inside my moped.

Apparently my brain doesn’t believe in separate compartments.

It just opens all the tabs at once.

Then there’s the new monitor.

I needed one. I bought one.

That part was thankfully uncomplicated.

The computer was slightly less so.

After selling my old machine to help pay for the moped, I found myself in the rather predictable position of needing another computer. So I’ve been collecting cheap parts again, gradually assembling something from whatever I can find for sensible money.

It’s not particularly glamorous, but I like building computers from mismatched collections of parts almost as much as I like fixing them, because there’s something satisfying about taking a collection of things that individually aren’t particularly useful and turning them into a machine that actually does something.

It also means I have another project.

Which, apparently, is important.

And then there is Bella.

She bullies me.

Everyone knows it. I know it. She knows it. I suspect she’d object to the phrasing, which is unfortunate because that only makes it more accurate.

She has somehow become the person I can tell about whatever ridiculous thing I’ve managed to get myself into, and despite the bullying, the teasing, and the occasional reminder that I am apparently incapable of making a sensible decision, I wouldn’t have it any other way.

I do, unfortunately, quite like her.

There, I’ve said it. She’ll hate that. Two can play at the game of bullying…

I’ve realised something over the last month that I’ve probably lived through dozens of times without putting a name to it. I fill space.

Whenever there is a gap in my schedule, I seem to find something to put in it. Sometimes that’s useful. Sometimes it’s productive. Sometimes it’s a five-dollar MacBook. Sometimes it’s an English assignment that I should have started three days earlier. Sometimes it’s a moped that I bought because I thought it would be a fun little project and have subsequently discovered contains significantly more electrical engineering than I was hoping for. I don’t necessarily think that’s a bad thing.

I’ve always liked being busy, and most of the things I do are things I genuinely enjoy, but there is a difference between having something to do and having something worth doing. I don’t always recognise that difference immediately.

Perhaps that’s why the last few weeks have felt so full. There has always been another problem to solve, another part to find, another thing to study, another machine to build, another message to send, another reason to keep moving.

And when everything is moving, it is remarkably easy not to notice that you’re tired.

The moped still doesn’t work properly, incidentally.

The stator is somewhere between here and wherever AliExpress has decided constitutes “nearby”. The regulator may or may not be dead. The battery is charged again, for now, and I am resisting the temptation to declare victory prematurely because apparently I’ve learned at least one lesson from all of this.

The computer is getting there. The English assignments are, technically, submitted. The exams are happening whether I’m ready for them or not. The phones are being fixed. And there are still five thousand things I’d like to do. So perhaps this really has just been a normal month.

A month of buying things I probably shouldn’t, repairing things that probably shouldn’t work, learning things I didn’t know I needed to learn, and spending far too much time trying to make a fifty cubic centimetre engine produce electricity.

It was supposed to be simple.

They always are.
